# Deep Research Prompt 2: Lyme Disease Patient and Clinician Journeys

## Role

Act as a multidisciplinary deep research team supporting early-stage product discovery for the **TopX Lyme Disease Challenge**.

Approach the work from the combined perspectives of:

* Patient experience research
* Clinical medicine
* Infectious disease
* Primary care
* Emergency and urgent care
* Diagnostic testing
* Public health
* Behavioral science
* Health-services research
* Human-centered design
* Health informatics
* Care navigation
* Responsible AI
* Digital health product strategy

Your assignment is to reconstruct how patients and clinicians actually experience Lyme disease-related decisions, not merely describe how ideal clinical guidelines say care should work.

---

# Primary objective

Create a comprehensive, evidence-grounded set of **patient and clinician journey maps** covering the major pathways associated with:

* Exposure prevention
* Tick encounters
* Early symptoms
* Initial care-seeking
* Clinical evaluation
* Diagnostic testing
* Treatment
* Follow-up
* Persistent or unexplained symptoms
* Referral and specialist care
* Public-health reporting
* Long-term care navigation

The research must reveal:

1. What people are trying to accomplish at each stage.
2. What decisions they must make.
3. What information they use.
4. What information they lack.
5. What actions they take.
6. Where delays, errors, misunderstandings, and handoff failures occur.
7. How patient and clinician perspectives differ.
8. How the experience varies across different patient populations and clinical settings.
9. What data is created, transferred, lost, or unavailable throughout the journey.
10. Which problems may warrant deeper investigation for an open-data and AI challenge.

This is a **journey discovery and workflow research assignment**.

Do not begin by designing a product, chatbot, diagnostic assistant, risk score, or clinical decision-support system.

Product implications may be discussed only after the journeys and their failure points have been documented.

---

# Challenge context

The research will support a team participating in the TopX Lyme Disease Challenge.

The team is in an early discovery phase and needs to understand:

* The lived experience of people navigating possible Lyme disease.
* The practical experience of clinicians evaluating and managing these patients.
* Differences between idealized clinical pathways and real-world care.
* Where patients, clinicians, laboratories, and public-health systems lose information.
* Where trust breaks down.
* Which decisions are time-sensitive.
* Which failure points cause the greatest harm, delay, cost, or frustration.
* Which journey problems could potentially be improved through public data, better data integration, or responsible AI.
* Which problems are fundamentally clinical, scientific, organizational, financial, or policy-related rather than technology problems.

The final report should help the team determine where to focus interviews, dataset research, and opportunity discovery.

It should not recommend a final challenge submission.

---

# Core research principle

Do not create one generalized “Lyme patient journey.”

Lyme disease experiences vary materially based on:

* Whether a tick was observed.
* Whether a rash was present.
* Whether the rash was typical or atypical.
* Whether symptoms developed quickly or gradually.
* Whether the patient lives in a high-incidence or emerging-risk area.
* Whether the clinician has substantial Lyme disease experience.
* Whether testing was performed at an appropriate time.
* Whether symptoms improved after treatment.
* Whether symptoms persisted or recurred.
* Whether the patient could access primary or specialist care.
* Whether the patient was a child, older adult, pregnant person, immunocompromised person, rural resident, or member of another population with distinct care needs.
* Whether Lyme disease was ultimately confirmed, considered probable, ruled out, misdiagnosed, or remained uncertain.

Construct multiple distinct journeys rather than averaging these experiences into one oversimplified pathway.

# Required journey archetypes

Research and reconstruct at least the following journeys.

Add additional archetypes when supported by evidence.

## Journey 1: Prevention before known exposure

A person who participates in outdoor work or recreation and is attempting to understand and reduce tick-borne disease risk.

Investigate:

* How the person assesses risk.
* Which information sources they use.
* Whether guidance is geographically and seasonally relevant.
* Prevention behaviors.
* Tick checks.
* Barriers to prevention.
* Differences between awareness and actual behavior.
* Risk communication failures.
* How often people underestimate or overestimate their risk.

Include examples such as:

* Hikers
* Campers
* Hunters
* Gardeners
* Outdoor workers
* Parents of children attending camps
* Pet owners
* Rural residents

---

## Journey 2: Known tick encounter without symptoms

A person finds a tick attached to themselves or another person but has no current symptoms.

Investigate:

* Tick removal.
* Tick preservation or disposal.
* Species identification.
* Estimating attachment duration.
* Assessing local disease risk.
* Deciding whether to contact a clinician.
* Deciding whether to test the tick.
* Understanding prophylactic treatment criteria.
* Monitoring for symptoms.
* Managing anxiety and uncertainty.
* Use of search engines, social media, public-health websites, pharmacists, urgent care, telehealth, and primary care.

Identify where the person receives conflicting or misleading information.

---

## Journey 3: Typical early presentation

A person develops a clinically recognizable early presentation, such as a characteristic rash, with or without a remembered tick bite.

Investigate:

* Symptom recognition.
* Care-seeking timing.
* Access to an appointment.
* Initial clinical evaluation.
* Whether testing is ordered.
* Treatment initiation.
* Patient education.
* Follow-up.
* Documentation.
* Reporting requirements.
* Differences between experienced and inexperienced clinicians.

Identify what makes this pathway relatively straightforward and where failure can still occur.

---

## Journey 4: Early symptoms without a remembered tick bite

A person develops nonspecific symptoms but does not remember a tick encounter.

Investigate:

* How the patient interprets symptoms.
* How clinicians develop the differential diagnosis.
* Whether geographic or recreational history is elicited.
* Whether the patient knows where exposure may have occurred.
* How Lyme disease enters or fails to enter the diagnostic reasoning process.
* The role of local incidence and clinician familiarity.
* Testing timing.
* Risk of premature reassurance.
* Risk of over-attributing nonspecific symptoms to Lyme disease.

---

## Journey 5: Atypical or absent rash

A person has Lyme disease-compatible symptoms but no classic rash, or a rash that is not recognized as typical.

Investigate:

* Patient recognition.
* Clinician recognition.
* Dermatologic differences and atypical presentations.
* Differences across skin tones.
* Use of photographs.
* Remote evaluation and telehealth.
* Differential diagnosis.
* Testing decisions.
* Delays and repeat visits.
* Referral patterns.
* Impact of documentation quality.

---

## Journey 6: Initial negative, equivocal, or conflicting testing

A patient is tested, but the result does not provide a clear answer.

Investigate:

* Timing of testing relative to symptom onset.
* Interpretation of negative results.
* Interpretation of equivocal results.
* Testing algorithms.
* Repeat testing.
* Differences among laboratories.
* Communication of uncertainty.
* Patient access to results.
* Portal messaging.
* Clinical follow-up.
* Patient interpretation of laboratory terminology.
* How test results are weighed against clinical findings.
* How patients seek second opinions or alternative testing.

Clearly distinguish guideline-supported testing from tests whose validity or interpretation is disputed.

---

## Journey 7: Delayed diagnosis or multiple clinical encounters

A patient experiences multiple visits before receiving a diagnosis or clear explanation.

Investigate:

* Initial symptoms.
* First point of care.
* Repeated urgent-care, emergency, or primary-care visits.
* Information lost between visits.
* Changing differential diagnoses.
* Lack of continuity.
* Testing repetition.
* Specialist referrals.
* Medical-record fragmentation.
* Geographic exposure history.
* Patient self-advocacy.
* Emotional and financial consequences.
* Factors associated with delay.

Do not assume that every delayed diagnosis represents clinical negligence. Identify scientific, presentation-related, access-related, and workflow-related contributors.

---

## Journey 8: Appropriate diagnosis and treatment with expected recovery

A patient is diagnosed, treated, and recovers without major ongoing complications.

Investigate:

* What information supports the successful pathway.
* How treatment expectations are communicated.
* Follow-up practices.
* Symptom monitoring.
* Return-to-work or activity decisions.
* Patient reassurance.
* Clinical documentation.
* Public-health reporting.
* Which factors make the journey work well.

Successful pathways are important because they reveal which capabilities should be preserved rather than redesigned.

---

## Journey 9: Persistent symptoms after treatment

A patient has ongoing fatigue, pain, cognitive complaints, or other functional symptoms after recommended treatment.

Investigate:

* Patient expectations about recovery.
* Follow-up with the original clinician.
* Assessment for alternative or additional diagnoses.
* Referral pathways.
* Symptom management.
* Repeated testing.
* Repeated treatment requests.
* Disagreement over terminology and causation.
* Patient-provider trust.
* Quality-of-life impact.
* Employment effects.
* Family and caregiver burden.
* Mental-health effects.
* Use of online communities.
* Use of complementary or unvalidated services.
* Gaps in longitudinal data and patient-reported outcomes.

Represent disagreement fairly and precisely.

Do not dismiss patient experiences because causation is uncertain.

Do not treat symptoms as proof of active infection when evidence does not establish that conclusion.

# Research methodology

## Source priorities

Prioritize sources in this order:

1. Peer-reviewed qualitative patient-experience research
2. Peer-reviewed clinician workflow and health-services research
3. Official federal, state, and local public-health sources
4. Major clinical guidelines and professional associations
5. Peer-reviewed epidemiological and diagnostic research
6. Official laboratory and testing documentation
7. Government-funded reports
8. Patient-reported outcome studies
9. Credible patient advocacy organizations
10. Published interviews and first-person patient accounts
11. Reputable journalism for contextual examples
12. Commercial sources only when analyzing actual products or workflows

Do not use isolated social-media posts as evidence of frequency or prevalence.

Social-media and forum content may be used to generate hypotheses about experiences, but it must be labeled accordingly.

---

## Qualitative evidence requirements

When using patient or clinician narratives:

* Identify whether the source is an individual anecdote, qualitative study, survey, or representative sample.
* Do not generalize a single experience to all patients.
* Preserve the distinction between lived experience and clinical causation.
* Identify recurring themes across multiple sources.
* Include both negative and successful experiences.
* Note sample-selection limitations.
* Avoid extracting quotations out of context.
* Do not expose private or identifying information.

---

## Ideal pathway versus real-world pathway

For each journey, explicitly compare:

### Expected or recommended pathway

What guidelines, official workflows, or clinical best practices indicate should happen.

### Observed real-world pathway

What research suggests often happens in practice.

### Gap

Where and why the observed journey differs from the expected journey.

Do not assume that the expected pathway is always achievable or that deviation necessarily indicates poor-quality care.

---

## Evidence classification

Classify major findings as:

* **Established evidence**
* **Strong but incomplete evidence**
* **Emerging evidence**
* **Expert consensus**
* **Qualitative recurring theme**
* **Patient-reported experience**
* **Clinician-reported experience**
* **Disputed**
* **Hypothesis**
* **Researcher inference**
* **Insufficient evidence**

---

## Contradictory evidence

For important claims:

* Search for evidence supporting the finding.
* Search for evidence challenging it.
* Explain whether differences arise from study population, geography, definitions, care setting, methodology, or time period.
* Clearly identify issues that remain unresolved.

---

## Safety and neutrality requirements

* Do not provide individualized medical advice.
* Do not diagnose.
* Do not recommend a specific treatment.
* Do not imply that AI should replace clinical judgment.
* Do not present patient frustration as proof of clinical error.
* Do not dismiss patient symptoms because diagnostic certainty is absent.
* Do not treat persistent symptoms as automatic evidence of active infection.
* Do not treat a surveillance definition as a clinical diagnostic rule.
* Do not equate a negative test with definitive exclusion in every clinical context.
* Do not characterize disputed testing or treatment approaches as validated without strong evidence.
* Do not frame clinicians and patients as opposing sides.
* Identify system, evidence, communication, and access failures without assigning blame unless supported by evidence.

# Required cross-journey synthesis

After documenting individual journeys, identify patterns that recur across multiple journeys.

At minimum, evaluate:

* Exposure history is not captured or transferred consistently.
* Geographic risk information is difficult to interpret.
* Patients cannot determine whether their situation is urgent.
* Clinicians lack time to reconstruct complex longitudinal histories.
* Testing uncertainty is poorly communicated.
* Negative results create different interpretations for patients and clinicians.
* Follow-up ownership is unclear.
* Patient-generated information is not integrated into clinical workflows.
* Specialist access is limited.
* Persistent symptoms create repeated loops through the care system.
* Public-health information arrives too late or at insufficient granularity.
* Patients and clinicians use different definitions of successful care.
* Trust deteriorates when uncertainty is not acknowledged.

For each pattern, state:

* Which journeys it appears in.
* Strength of evidence.
* Root cause.
* Consequence.
* Whether it is likely to be addressable.
* What research should happen next.

Do not assume the listed patterns are true. Confirm, reject, or refine them based on evidence.

# Quality-control checklist

Before finalizing the report, verify that:

* Multiple distinct journeys were created rather than one average journey.
* Both successful and unsuccessful journeys were analyzed.
* Patient, clinician, laboratory, and public-health perspectives were included.
* Every major journey includes decisions, actions, information, data, handoffs, pain points, and outcomes.
* Expected pathways were compared with observed real-world pathways.
* Patient experiences were represented respectfully.
* Clinical uncertainty was represented accurately.
* Disputed claims were labeled.
* Surveillance and clinical diagnosis were not conflated.
* Technology was not treated as the default solution.
* Public-data uses were separated into population-level and individual-level applications.
* AI opportunities include risks and non-AI alternatives.
* Major assumptions were converted into interview hypotheses.
* The output ends with a concrete discovery backlog rather than a product concept.
